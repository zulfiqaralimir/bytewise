## Why This Guide Exists

Most founders treat the financial model as a box to check before an investor meeting — a
spreadsheet full of numbers nobody, including the founder, can defend under questioning.

This guide documents a different approach: build the model **tab by tab, in a fixed
dependency order**, verify each tab before moving to the next, and let the final Balance
Sheet prove — not assert — that the numbers hold together.

It was written up after building a real 3-year financial model for **ZulfiQode**, the
crisis-prediction AI product from Black Iron Quantum AI (Private) Limited, following the
structure taught in a National Incubation Center (NIC) Islamabad financial modeling
workshop. The goal here is to turn that one real build into a reusable template for the
next startup, the next pitch, the next model.

---

## Before You Open Excel

Have three things ready:

1. **A revenue logic you can explain in one sentence.** For ZulfiQode: *"corporate clients
   pay an annual license; smaller funds pay a monthly subscription."* If you can't state
   your revenue logic that simply, the model will be guesswork dressed up as precision.
2. **A reference model or workshop template**, if one exists. Don't reinvent the tab
   structure — reuse a proven one and adapt the numbers.
3. **A willingness to guess, mark the guess, and fix it later.** Nobody has real client
   counts and pricing before they've closed a client. The discipline is not in having real
   numbers on day one — it's in making every guess *visible* (so it can be corrected) and
   never hard-coding a guess where a formula should be.

---

## The Tab-by-Tab Blueprint

Build tabs in this order — each one depends on the tab before it, so building out of order
means broken links or, worse, numbers that look plausible but are quietly wrong.

| # | Tab | Depends on | Produces |
|---|-----|-----------|----------|
| 1 | **Assumptions** | Nothing | Revenue and cost drivers, month by month |
| 2 | **Salary** | Nothing (headcount is its own input) | Total payroll cost |
| 3 | **Depreciation** | Nothing (asset register is its own input) | Depreciation expense, equipment book value |
| 4 | **Income Statement** | Assumptions, Salary, Depreciation | Net Profit |
| 5 | **Balance Sheet** | Assumptions, Depreciation, Income Statement | Assets, Liabilities, Equity (cash still a placeholder) |
| 6 | **Cashflow Statement** | Income Statement, Balance Sheet, Depreciation | Real cash balance — linked back into the Balance Sheet |
| 7 | **Dashboard** | Everything above | One-page summary for investors |

Notice tab 5 is deliberately incomplete when you build it — more on that below.

## Step 1 — Assumptions: Revenue Drivers

This is the tab everything else depends on, so get the *structure* right even if the
*numbers* are placeholders.

For each revenue stream, build a chain of four rows, left to right across your time
periods (we used 36 monthly columns):

1. **New units added this period** (input) — new clients signed, new subscribers joined
2. **Cumulative units** (formula) — a running total: `=previous_month + this_month's_new`
3. **Price per unit** (input) — can escalate year over year
4. **Revenue this period** (formula) — `= cumulative units × price`

Repeat this chain for every revenue stream, then sum them into a **Total Revenue** row.

**Practical tip:** if you're not sure how a contract is billed (upfront vs. spread across
the year), default to spreading it monthly. It's simpler, and it matches how most
subscription and model tabs are built anyway.

**Color convention** (carry this through every tab):
- **Blue text** = input, safe to edit
- **Black text** = formula, don't overtype
- **Yellow fill** = a key assumption worth a second look before you present

---

## Step 2 — Cost Assumptions: COGS + the Salary Tab

Split costs into two categories, because they behave differently:

- **Costs that scale with customers** (data feeds, cloud/API costs, hosting) belong in the
  Assumptions tab as a "cost per unit" row, multiplied by the same cumulative-client rows
  from Step 1.
- **Costs that scale with your team** (salaries) get their own tab, because a team has
  structure — multiple roles, headcount changes, salary escalation — that clutters a
  general assumptions tab.

**Salary tab structure:**
1. Headcount by role, by month (input)
2. Salary per person, by role, by month (input, can escalate yearly)
3. Total cost per role (formula = headcount × salary)
4. Grand total salary expense (formula = sum of all roles)

Also add, while you're in the Assumptions tab: a **marketing expense** (often simplest as
a % of revenue), a **fixed G&A cost** (rent, legal, admin), and a **bad-debt %** (a small
buffer for late or missed payments — enterprise clients are usually more reliable than
retail, so this can be small, 2–3%).

---

## Step 3 — Depreciation & Capex

Any equipment you buy (laptops, servers, office setup) isn't a one-time expense on the
Income Statement — it's spread over its useful life. Build this as two blocks:

**Asset register** (a small static table):

| Asset | Purchase Month | Price | Useful Life (months) | Monthly Depreciation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laptops (batch 1) | 1 | 1,000,000 | 36 | =Price / Life |

**Monthly schedule**, driven off that register:
- Depreciation expense per asset per month:
  `=IF(AND(month >= purchase_month, month < purchase_month + life), monthly_depreciation, 0)`
- Total depreciation this month = sum across all assets
- Accumulated depreciation = running total
- **Net Book Value** = *(cost of everything purchased so far)* − accumulated depreciation

### The mistake we made here — and how it showed up later

The first version of this tab calculated Net Book Value as:

> Total cost of **every asset in the register** − accumulated depreciation

That's wrong if some assets haven't been purchased yet. It silently counted equipment we
hadn't bought yet (a Year 2 and a Year 3 laptop batch) as if it already existed on the
books. The fix:

> Net Book Value = cost of assets **purchased by this month** − accumulated depreciation

using `SUMPRODUCT((purchase_month_range <= this_month) * price_range)` instead of a fixed
total. This single-line bug created a PKR 1,500,000 phantom asset that didn't surface until
the Balance Sheet refused to balance three steps later — which is exactly why the next two
steps matter.

## Step 4 — Income Statement

This is where everything built so far comes together:

```
Revenue                              (from Assumptions)
(–) Cost of Revenue (COGS)           (from Assumptions)
= Gross Profit

(–) Marketing Expense                (from Assumptions)
(–) Salary Expense                   (from Salary tab)
(–) G&A Expense                      (from Assumptions)
(–) Bad Debts                        (from Assumptions)
(–) Depreciation Expense             (from Depreciation tab)
= Total Operating Expenses

Gross Profit – Total Operating Expenses = Profit Before Tax

Tax = IF(Profit Before Tax > 0, PBT × tax rate, 0)   ← never tax a loss

Profit Before Tax – Tax = Net Profit
```

**Color convention addition:** use a different color (we used green) for any number that
links in from another tab, versus black for a formula calculated within the same tab. It
makes it instantly obvious, months later, where a number actually comes from.

---

## Step 5 — Balance Sheet (Yes, Leave Cash Blank)

Build everything on the Balance Sheet **except Cash**, and mark Cash clearly as a
placeholder (we set it to 0 with an orange/highlighted fill and a note). This mirrors how
the numbers actually get built in practice — you can't know the real cash balance until
you've built the Cashflow Statement, so don't pretend otherwise.

```
ASSETS
  Cash                     ← placeholder (0), to be linked in Step 6
  Accounts Receivable      = Revenue × (Days Sales Outstanding ÷ 30)
  Equipment, Net Book Value  (linked from Depreciation)
  = Total Assets

LIABILITIES
  Accounts Payable         = COGS × (Days Payable Outstanding ÷ 30)
  = Total Liabilities

EQUITY
  Paid-in Capital           (input — your seed/founder investment)
  Retained Earnings        = running total of Net Profit from the Income Statement
  = Total Equity
```

Add one more row: a **Balance Check** = Total Assets − (Total Liabilities + Equity). With
Cash at 0, this row won't be zero — and that's useful information. It's telling you exactly
how much cash the business needs at each point in time to stay solvent. Don't hide this
number; it previews the story the Cashflow Statement is about to tell.

---

## Step 6 — Cashflow Statement (and Closing the Loop)

Build this using the **indirect method** — starting from Net Profit and adjusting for
non-cash items and working-capital changes:

```
OPERATING ACTIVITIES
  Net Profit                              (from Income Statement)
  (+) Depreciation                        (non-cash, from Depreciation tab)
  (–) Increase in Accounts Receivable     (change in AR, from Balance Sheet)
  (+) Increase in Accounts Payable        (change in AP, from Balance Sheet)
  = Net Cash from Operating Activities

INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  (–) Purchase of Equipment (Capex)       (from the Depreciation asset register,
                                            matched to the actual purchase month)
  = Net Cash from Investing Activities

FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  (+) Paid-in Capital raised this month   (change in capital, from Balance Sheet)
  = Net Cash from Financing Activities

Net Change in Cash = Operating + Investing + Financing
Beginning Cash = last month's Ending Cash (0 in month 1)
Ending Cash = Beginning Cash + Net Change in Cash
```

**Now go back and fix the Balance Sheet:** replace the Cash placeholder with a link to
`Ending Cash` from this tab. If Steps 1–6 were all done correctly, the **Balance Check row
on the Balance Sheet should now read exactly 0 in every single month.**

This is the real test of the entire model — not "does it run without errors," but "does it
balance." A model with formula errors is obviously broken. A model that *runs fine* but
doesn't balance is far more dangerous, because it looks trustworthy right up until someone
asks the wrong question in a due-diligence meeting.

---

## Step 7 — Dashboard

Once everything ties out, build a one-page summary — this is what gets glanced at first,
whether by you, a mentor, or an investor:

- **Key metrics strip**: total 3-year revenue, total 3-year net profit, ending cash,
  breakeven month, lowest cash point (this tells you your minimum funding need), and
  headline client/user counts.
- **Annual summary table**: Year 1 / Year 2 / Year 3 side by side for Revenue, COGS, Gross
  Profit, OpEx, Net Profit, Ending Cash — the story at a glance.
- **Trend charts**: monthly revenue, monthly net profit, and cash balance across the full
  period. Native spreadsheet charts (not screenshots) so they update automatically when you
  change an assumption.

A useful formula trick for the "breakeven month" metric, without needing an array formula:
`=COUNTIF(PBT_range, "<=0") + 1` — this counts the unprofitable months and adds one,
which works cleanly as long as profitability, once achieved, doesn't dip negative again.

## The Reusable Checklist

Use this on the next model, whatever the business:

- [ ] State your revenue logic in one sentence before opening Excel
- [ ] Build tabs in dependency order: Assumptions → Salary → Depreciation → Income
      Statement → Balance Sheet → Cashflow Statement → Dashboard
- [ ] Every revenue/cost driver follows: new-this-period → cumulative → price/cost →
      revenue/expense
- [ ] Color-code consistently: blue = input, black = same-tab formula, green = cross-tab
      link, yellow fill = key assumption to review
- [ ] Leave Cash as a marked placeholder on the Balance Sheet until the Cashflow Statement
      is built
- [ ] Add a Balance Check row and don't consider the model done until it reads 0 everywhere
- [ ] Never hard-code a number that should be a formula — even a "final" number like total
      capex should sum from its inputs, so a later correction cascades automatically
- [ ] Recalculate and scan for formula errors after every tab, not just at the end
- [ ] Build the Dashboard last, once every number underneath it is proven correct

---

## Closing Note

Treat every number in this model as provisional. The structure is the durable part — the
dependency order, the formula discipline, the balance check. The actual client counts,
pricing, and salaries should be replaced the moment real conversations (with a client, a
ministry contact, an investor) produce a firmer number. That's not a flaw in the model —
it's the model doing its job: giving you a living structure that gets more accurate every
time you feed it real information, instead of a static document that's wrong the day after
you print it.
