# Introduction to CGE Modelling — Part 11: CES Elasticities of Substitution

> *Economics & Finance Models · CGE Modelling Series · Part 11 of 13*

Parts 9 and 10 walked through Model 0 and Model EXTER, both built on Cobb-Douglas production. This part explains why Cobb-Douglas is a special case, and what changes when sectors substitute labour for capital at different rates.

---

## Beyond Cobb-Douglas: CES Elasticities of Substitution

Model 0's Cobb-Douglas production function assumes a fixed elasticity of substitution (σ = 1) between labour and capital. Real economies vary by sector, which is where the **CES (Constant Elasticity of Substitution)** function comes in.

$$\sigma_i = \frac{\% \text{ change in } (K/L)}{\% \text{ change in } (w/r)}$$

In plain terms: when the wage rises 1%, how much do firms substitute capital for labour?

- **High σ** (easy to substitute): a 10% wage rise might cut labour demand 15% (σ ≈ 1.5) as firms switch readily to machines.
- **Medium σ**: a 10% wage rise cuts labour demand ≈ 8% (σ ≈ 0.8).
- **Low σ** (hard to substitute): a 10% wage rise cuts labour demand only ≈ 3% (σ ≈ 0.3).

### Pakistan Sector Elasticities

| Sector | σᵢ | Labour Share αᵢ | Substitution | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 0.67 | 0.40 | Low | Hard to automate farm labour |
| Manufacturing | 0.83 | 0.25 | Moderate | Some processes can be automated |
| Services | 1.00 | 0.45 | Balanced | Mix of human and machine service delivery |
| Energy | 0.56 | 0.10 | Very Low | Rigid production process |

The general form for agriculture, for instance, is:

$$XS_{AG} = A_{AG}\left[\delta_{AG}(LD_{AG})^{-\rho} + (1-\delta_{AG})(KD_{AG})^{-\rho}\right]^{-1/\rho}, \qquad \sigma_{AG} = \frac{1}{1+\rho}$$

Approximating the labour-demand response to a wage change as $\%\Delta LD_i \approx -\sigma_i \times \%\Delta w$:

**A 10% wage increase across sectors:**

| Sector | σᵢ | Employment Loss | Multiplier Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 0.67 | 6.7% | High |
| Manufacturing | 0.83 | 8.3% | Medium |
| Services | 1.00 | 10.0% | Medium-Low |
| Energy | 0.56 | 5.6% | Very High |

**Key insight:**

$$\text{Low } \sigma \implies \text{High Multiplier}$$

Low elasticity means firms cannot easily substitute machines for labour. When wages rise, workers keep their jobs, keep earning, and keep spending — so the multiplier effect on the rest of the economy stays strong. High elasticity means the opposite: employment adjusts more, and the multiplier is weaker.

### <span style={{ color: "#2563eb", fontWeight: 700 }}>Before and After: Actual Headcounts</span>

The percentages above are easier to feel in actual worker counts. Model EXTER's SAM (Part 10) reports 5,760 Agriculture workers, 7,560 Industry workers, 8,580 Services workers, and 6,960 Non-tradables workers at the benchmark. Applying $\%\Delta LD_i \approx -\sigma_i \times \%\Delta w$ for a 10% wage rise:

| Sector | Workers Before | $\sigma_i$ | Workers After | Jobs Lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 5,760 | 0.67 | ≈ 5,374 | 386 |
| Manufacturing | 7,560 | 0.83 | ≈ 6,933 | 627 |
| Services | 8,580 | 1.00 | 7,722 | 858 |
| Energy | 6,960 | 0.56 | ≈ 6,570 | 390 |

Agriculture and Energy — the two low-σ sectors — shed the fewest jobs in absolute terms despite starting from similar headcounts to Manufacturing and Services, which is exactly the "low σ ⇒ high multiplier" relationship above showing up as real employment.

### Where Elasticities Actually Come From

Elasticities are not estimated *within* the CGE model itself — the model takes them as given inputs. They're sourced from outside the model in five ways:

```text
Sources of Elasticities:

1. Econometric Studies (most common)
   ├─ Researchers estimate from historical data
   └─ "When wages rose 10%, employment fell X%"

2. Literature Review
   ├─ Copy from published papers
   └─ "Other economists found σ = 0.67 for agriculture"

3. Calibration from SAM
   ├─ Use SAM data + assumptions
   └─ "If output doubled, would labour need to double?"

4. Expert Judgment
   ├─ "Agriculture can't automate easily"
   └─ Set σ = 0.67 based on expert opinion

5. International Databases
   ├─ GTAP (Global Trade Analysis Project)
   └─ World Bank, IMF databases
```

In practice, most country CGE studies blend these: start from a GTAP or literature value, then adjust it with expert judgment to fit local structural conditions before locking it in at the calibration stage described in [Part 4](/blog/introduction-to-cge-modelling-part-4).

### Worked Example: Econometric Estimation

A researcher studying Pakistan agriculture from 1990 to 2020 collects wage and employment data:

```text
Data collected:
├─ Year 1990: Wage = 100, Employment = 1,000
├─ Year 2000: Wage = 150, Employment = 950
├─ Year 2010: Wage = 200, Employment = 920
└─ Year 2020: Wage = 250, Employment = 900

Calculate elasticity:
% Δ Wage = (250-100)/100 = 150%
% Δ Employment = (900-1000)/1000 = -10%

σ = |% Δ Employment| / |% Δ Wage|
σ = 10% / 150% = 0.067 ≈ 0.67
```

**Conclusion:** agriculture's elasticity of substitution ≈ 0.67 — the same value used in the sector table above.

### Worked Example: Literature Review

Instead of running the regression yourself, you search for existing estimates and average them:

```text
Paper 1 (2010): "Production elasticity in Pakistan manufacturing"
├─ Estimates σ = 0.85
└─ 500 firms studied

Paper 2 (2015): "Factor substitution in developing countries"
├─ Estimates σ = 0.80 for manufacturing
└─ Cross-country analysis

Paper 3 (2020): "Pakistan industrial survey"
├─ Estimates σ = 0.83
└─ Recent data

Average: (0.85 + 0.80 + 0.83) / 3 = 0.83

Use: σ_manufacturing = 0.83
```

### Worked Example: Calibration from the SAM

Where no direct estimate exists, you can infer a plausible elasticity from the SAM's own factor shares plus a modelling judgment call:

```text
From SAM:
├─ Agriculture: 5,760 labour, 1,440 capital
├─ Labour share: α = 5,760 / (5,760+1,440) = 0.80
└─ Capital share: (1-α) = 0.20

Calibration logic:
"If production doubles, would labour and capital both double?"
├─ YES → σ = 1.0 (Cobb-Douglas)
├─ NO, labour needs less → σ > 1.0 (high substitution)
└─ NO, labour needs more → σ < 1.0 (low substitution)

For agriculture: assume σ = 0.67 (a reasonable guess given the SAM's labour intensity)
```

### A Typical Workflow for a Pakistan CGE Model

```text
Step 1: Literature Review
├─ Search papers on Pakistan agriculture, manufacturing, etc.
└─ Collect elasticity estimates from 5-10 papers

Step 2: Average Estimates
├─ If estimates are: 0.65, 0.68, 0.70, 0.66
└─ Average = 0.67

Step 3: Sensitivity Analysis
├─ Build model with σ = 0.67
├─ Also test with σ = 0.60 and σ = 0.75
└─ See if results change

Step 4: Use Best Estimate
├─ If σ = 0.67 matches Pakistan best
└─ Use it in model
```

### Why Elasticities Differ Across Sectors

Different sectors substitute labour for capital at different rates because they run on different technologies:

```text
Agriculture:
├─ Technology: Traditional farming
├─ Substitution: Hard (need workers)
└─ σ = 0.67 (low)

Manufacturing:
├─ Technology: Assembly line, robots possible
├─ Substitution: Easier
└─ σ = 0.83 (medium)

Services:
├─ Technology: Mixed (humans + computers)
├─ Substitution: Balanced
└─ σ = 1.0 (balanced)

Energy:
├─ Technology: Refineries, rigid process
├─ Substitution: Very hard
└─ σ = 0.56 (very low)
```

### Sensitivity Checks

Once an elasticity is chosen, it's worth stress-testing it before locking it into the model:

```text
Does σ_agriculture = 0.67 make sense?

Test 1: When wage ↑ 20%
├─ Employment ↓ 13.4% (using σ = 0.67)
└─ Does this match Pakistan reality?

Test 2: Compare to other countries
├─ India agriculture σ = 0.70 (similar)
├─ Brazil agriculture σ = 0.65 (similar)
└─ Pakistan σ = 0.67 seems reasonable

Test 3: Expert opinion
├─ Ask Pakistan agricultural economists
└─ "Does this elasticity match reality?"
```

**Summary — where elasticities come from:**

- ✅ **Primary:** econometric estimation from historical data
- ✅ **Secondary:** literature review of published papers
- ✅ **Tertiary:** SAM calibration and expert judgment

**For a Pakistan CGE model specifically:**

1. Search GTAP, World Bank, and Pakistan Bureau of Statistics databases
2. Review Pakistani academic papers
3. Average estimates from multiple sources
4. Test with sensitivity analysis
5. Validate with domain experts
