# Introduction to CGE Modelling — Part 5: Running Policy Simulations

> *Economics & Finance Models · CGE Modelling Series · Part 5 of 13*

Part 4 covered calibrating a model to a base year. This part covers what you actually do with a calibrated model: run policy simulations.

---

## Running Policy Simulations

Once calibrated, a CGE model lets you simulate "what-if" policy scenarios.

### A. How Simulations Work

1. **Baseline** — the calibrated model at equilibrium
2. **Shock** — change a policy variable (tax rate, tariff, subsidy, etc.)
3. **Adjustment** — prices and quantities change until a new equilibrium is reached
4. **Comparison** — new equilibrium vs. baseline = policy impact

### B. Types of Policy Shocks

- **Tax policy** — VAT changes, carbon tax, income tax
- **Trade policy** — removing tariffs, import quotas, currency devaluation
- **Subsidy changes** — removing fuel subsidies, agricultural support
- **Productivity shocks** — technology improvements, infrastructure investment
- **Demographic changes** — population growth, labour force changes

### C. Example — Removing an Import Tariff

Initial situation: 20% tariff on imported textiles. Shock: remove the tariff.

1. Imported textiles become cheaper
2. Consumers buy more imports, less domestic textiles
3. Domestic textile producers reduce output
4. Workers are laid off from the textile sector
5. Workers move to other sectors (if labour is mobile)
6. Wages may adjust downward
7. Government loses tariff revenue
8. Consumers benefit from lower prices
9. A new equilibrium is reached

### D. Reading the Results

The model reports changes in GDP, sectoral output, employment by sector, prices, trade balance, household welfare (often "equivalent variation"), government revenue, and income distribution.

### E. Comparative Statics vs. Dynamic Models

**Comparative statics** (most common) compares two equilibria — before and after the shock — with no time dimension; it answers "what's the long-run effect?" **Dynamic CGE** shows the adjustment path over time, answering "how does the economy transition year by year?"

### F. Interpretation Caution

Results show direction and relative magnitude, not precise forecasts. "GDP increases by 2.3%" means a positive, moderate effect — useful for comparing policies against each other, not as an exact prediction.

### G. Sensitivity Analysis

Because elasticities are uncertain, good practice is to re-run simulations with different elasticity values, check whether conclusions hold, and identify which parameters matter most.
