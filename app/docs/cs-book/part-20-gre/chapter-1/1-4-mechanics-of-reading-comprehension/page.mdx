# <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Mechanics of Reading Comprehension</span>

Just as Grammar Mechanics ([Lesson 3.2](/docs/cs-book/part-20-gre/chapter-3/3-2-grammar-mechanics-checklist)) governs how you construct sentences correctly for <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Analytical Writing</span>, Reading Mechanics governs how you process a passage fast enough to answer questions <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>without re-reading from scratch</span>. This lesson lays out the <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>six core rules</span>, then gives you a timed passage to drill them.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>The Six Mechanics of Reading Comprehension</span>

### 1. Structural Mapping

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Read each paragraph once, and after finishing it, mentally label its *function* in 3–6 words</span> — not its content.

> Para 1 = "states the problem" | Para 2 = "gives counter-view" | Para 3 = "offers resolution"

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>This map is what you consult when a question hits, instead of re-reading the whole passage from scratch.</span>

### 2. Signal Word Tracking

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Certain words</span> tell you what's about <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>to happen in the next clause</span> *before* you finish reading it — read them as <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>signposts, not just connectors.</span>

| Signal Type | Words | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | however, but, although, yet, despite | <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Next idea opposes the previous one</span> |
| Continuation | moreover, furthermore, similarly | <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Next idea supports/extends the previous one</span> |
| Cause-Effect | because, therefore, thus, consequently | <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Next idea explains or results from the previous one</span> |

Spotting these lets you <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>predict sentence direction instead of processing every word with equal effort.</span>

### 3. Skip Density Reading

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Not every sentence deserves equal attention.</span>

- **Skim on first pass:** <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>names, dates, specific numbers, minor examples — you can return to these for Detail questions.</span>
- **Read fully:** <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>claims, opinions, and transition sentences — these are what Main Idea, Inference, and Structure questions target.</span>

### 4. Question-First Targeting

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>For Detail, Inference, and Vocabulary-in-Context questions, read the question *before* re-scanning the passage.</span> The question usually <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>names or implies which paragraph holds the answer</span> — use your structural map to <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>jump straight there</span> instead of re-reading everything.

### 5. Elimination Over Confirmation

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Instead of searching for why an answer choice is *right* (slow, effortful), search for why each of the other four are *wrong* (usually faster).</span> <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Wrong answers typically have one clear flaw:</span>

- Contradicts the passage
- Too extreme ("always," "never," "completely")
- Out of scope (not mentioned at all)
- Right idea, wrong paragraph/detail

### 6. No Re-Reading Rule (Practice Constraint)

During practice, force yourself to read the passage exactly once before looking at any question — no going back for a "second full read." This trains your brain to extract structure on the first pass, since it knows a second read isn't coming. (In the actual exam, you'll still glance back — but only to the *specific sentence* your map points to, not the whole passage.)

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Timed Practice Passage</span>

**Instructions:** Set a timer for 2 minutes. Read the passage once, applying Mechanics 1–3 (map each paragraph's function, track signal words, skim names/dates). Do not re-read. Then answer the questions below, applying Mechanics 4–5 (go straight to the right paragraph, eliminate wrong answers).

> Historians have long debated the primary cause of the decline of the Indus Valley Civilization, one of the world's earliest urban cultures. Early twentieth-century scholars favored the theory of a large-scale invasion by Indo-Aryan peoples, pointing to skeletal remains found in the ruins of Mohenjo-daro as evidence of violent conflict. However, subsequent archaeological analysis revealed that these remains were scattered across different time periods and showed no consistent pattern of warfare-related trauma, undermining the invasion theory considerably. More recent research has instead pointed to climate change as the likely primary factor: geological evidence suggests that the Ghaggar-Hakra river system, on which many Indus Valley settlements depended, gradually dried up over several centuries. This slow environmental shift, researchers argue, would have driven agricultural decline and gradual urban abandonment far more plausibly than a sudden military conquest. Still, some scholars caution against attributing the civilization's decline to a single cause, noting that trade disruption, changes in monsoon patterns, and internal social shifts likely occurred simultaneously and reinforced one another.

### Questions

**1. (Main Idea)** Which of the following best expresses the main point of the passage?

A) The Indus Valley Civilization was destroyed by an Indo-Aryan invasion.
B) Skeletal evidence definitively proves warfare caused the civilization's decline.
C) Scholarly understanding of the Indus Valley Civilization's decline has shifted from invasion theory toward multi-causal explanations centered on environmental change.
D) The Ghaggar-Hakra river system was the sole cause of the civilization's collapse.
E) Monsoon pattern changes are the most recent and widely accepted explanation.

**2. (Structure)** The third sentence ("However, subsequent archaeological analysis...") primarily serves to:

A) Introduce a new, unrelated theory.
B) Undermine the credibility of the invasion theory presented earlier.
C) Confirm the accuracy of the skeletal evidence.
D) Explain the causes of climate change.
E) Summarize the passage's overall conclusion.

**3. (Detail)** According to the passage, what evidence is cited in support of the climate change theory?

A) Skeletal remains showing trauma patterns
B) Trade route disruption records
C) Geological evidence of the Ghaggar-Hakra river system drying up
D) Monsoon pattern records from the period
E) Urban abandonment records from Mohenjo-daro

**4. (Inference)** It can be inferred from the passage that the scholars mentioned in the final sentence would most likely agree that:

A) Climate change was the only meaningful factor in the civilization's decline.
B) The invasion theory remains the most credible explanation.
C) A combination of interacting factors better explains the decline than any single cause.
D) Trade disruption alone caused the civilization's collapse.
E) The skeletal evidence from Mohenjo-daro was ultimately conclusive.

---

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Answer Key</span>

1. **C** — The passage traces a shift from invasion theory → climate change theory → a multi-causal view. (A) and (D) are too narrow/extreme; (B) contradicts the passage; (E) misrepresents "recent" as the sole final answer when the passage actually ends on multi-causality.

2. **B** — Signal word "However" flags a contrast with the previous invasion-theory claim; the sentence goes on to describe evidence that "undermin[ed] the invasion theory considerably."

3. **C** — Directly stated: "geological evidence suggests that the Ghaggar-Hakra river system... gradually dried up."

4. **C** — The final sentence explicitly states these scholars believe multiple factors "occurred simultaneously and reinforced one another" — a direct paraphrase, not a leap.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Scoring Your Speed</span>

- **Read the passage in under 60 seconds** + **answer all 4 questions correctly**: strong pace — you're applying the mechanics well.
- **Read under 60 seconds but missed 1–2 questions**: your speed is fine, but you're skimming claims/transitions too lightly (Mechanic #3) — slow down slightly on those, not on names/dates.
- **Took the full 2 minutes or more just to read**: you're still reading for full content retention rather than structure — repeat this same passage tomorrow without looking at the questions, focusing only on producing paragraph labels quickly.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Practice Method</span>

1. Repeat this drill with a new short passage (150–250 words) daily.
2. Track two numbers each time: **reading time** and **questions correct**.
3. Goal by end of month: consistently read a 200-word passage in **under 60 seconds** while maintaining 80%+ accuracy.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Key Takeaway</span>

Reading speed on the GRE isn't about reading faster in general — it's about reading *selectively*: full attention on claims and transitions, light attention on names/dates/examples, and zero re-reading of the whole passage once your structural map exists. Speed is a byproduct of good mechanics, not a separate skill to force.
