# <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}><span style={{ color: "#059669" }}>Analytical Writing Decoded</span>: The Issue Task</span>

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Analytical Writing is the third section of the GRE, alongside Verbal and Quantitative Reasoning.</span> As of the current (shortened) GRE format, it consists of a single task: the **Analyze an Issue** task. The older "Analyze an Argument" task has been removed from the exam.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Overview</span>

- **Format:** 1 essay task
- **Time:** <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>30 minutes — no separate outline time, planning, writing, and revising all happen within the 30 minutes.</span>
- **Score range:** 0–6, in half-point increments
- **Unlike Verbal:** <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>this section does test grammar and sentence-level writing mechanics, not just logic and vocabulary.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>What the Task Asks</span>

You're given a <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>general statement or claim on a broad topic</span> — education, government, technology, society, culture — and asked to <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree</span> with it, <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>supporting your position with reasons and examples.</span>

### Sample Prompt

> "Governments should invest more in public transportation than in building new roads."
>
> Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the statement above, and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true, and explain how these considerations shape your response.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>What Is Scored</span>

| Criterion | What Graders Look For |
|---|---|
| **Logical structure** | <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>A clear position, stated early, with an argument that develops logically from point to point</span> |
| **Supporting reasons/examples** | <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Specific, relevant reasons and examples — not vague generalities</span> |
| **Complexity / nuance** | <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Acknowledging counterarguments or exceptions, rather than a one-sided, unqualified stance</span> |
| **Grammar & mechanics** | <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Sentence control, clarity, and correctness</span> — this is the one GRE section where writing mechanics are directly graded |

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>A high-scoring essay is not necessarily the one with the "correct" opinion — there is no correct opinion.</span> <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>It's the one that argues its position clearly, supports it with real reasoning, and shows awareness of complexity.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Timing Strategy (30 Minutes)</span>

Because there's no separate planning phase, <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>a fast mental structure matters more than polishing every sentence:</span>

1. **~3 minutes — Plan:** Decide your position. <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Jot 2–3 supporting reasons and one counterpoint you'll acknowledge.</span>
2. **~22 minutes — Write:** Introduction (state position) → 2–3 body paragraphs (one reason + example each) → <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>brief paragraph acknowledging a counterpoint or limitation</span> → conclusion.
3. **~5 minutes — Revise:** Check for clarity, obvious grammar errors, and that your conclusion actually matches your stated position.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Sample Response Walkthrough</span>

**Prompt (from above):** *"Governments should invest more in public transportation than in building new roads."*

**Position taken:** Largely agree, with a stated exception.

**Structure used:**
- **Paragraph 1 (Introduction):** States agreement with the claim, with a qualification — investment priority should depend on a city's existing infrastructure and density.
- **Paragraph 2 (Reason 1):** Public transportation investment yields greater long-term capacity gains in dense urban areas, since it moves more people per mile of infrastructure than roads.
- **Paragraph 3 (Reason 2):** Environmental and congestion externalities of road expansion (induced demand, emissions) are avoided by transit investment.
- **Paragraph 4 (Counterpoint acknowledgment):** In low-density or rural regions, road infrastructure may still be the more practical priority, since transit ridership there is unlikely to justify the investment.
- **Paragraph 5 (Conclusion):** Reaffirms the qualified agreement — transit investment should be prioritized specifically in dense urban contexts, not universally.

**Why this scores well:** <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>It takes a clear position, supports it with two distinct reasons (not just restatements of the same point), and explicitly engages with a counterpoint instead of ignoring it</span> — which directly maps to the "complexity/nuance" scoring criterion.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Grammar & Mechanics Checklist</span>

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Verbal Reasoning (Text Completion, Sentence Equivalence, Reading Comprehension) never tests grammar directly — but Analytical Writing does.</span> Here's what graders actually pay attention to:

### 1. Sentence Structure & Variety
- Avoid run-on sentences and fragments.
- Mix simple, compound, and complex sentences — not just short choppy ones or one long tangled one.
- Use subordinate clauses (*although, because, while*, etc.) correctly to show logical relationships.

### 2. Subject-Verb Agreement
- Watch for cases where the subject and verb are separated by a clause.
- Example: "The *benefits* of the policy, despite its cost, *remain* significant" (not "remains").

### 3. Verb Tense Consistency
- Don't shift between past and present tense without reason within the same argument.

### 4. Pronoun Clarity
- Every "it," "this," "they" should have one obvious antecedent.
- Vague pronoun reference is one of the most common issues that costs clarity points.

### 5. Parallel Structure
- Lists and comparisons should use matching grammatical forms.
- Example: "The policy would reduce congestion, improve air quality, and *saving* costs" should be "*save* costs" — parallel with "reduce" and "improve."

### 6. Punctuation
- Avoid comma splices (joining two full sentences with just a comma).
- Use semicolons correctly for closely related independent clauses.
- Not essential to be perfect, but frequent errors here signal weak control.

### 7. Word Choice & Precision
- Use words correctly, not just impressively (e.g., "affect" vs. "effect," "fewer" vs. "less").
- Overly fancy vocabulary used incorrectly hurts more than plain, correct vocabulary.

### What Graders Are NOT Nitpicking
- A few minor typos or occasional small errors won't tank your score.
- This isn't a copyediting test — perfect academic-journal-level prose isn't the bar.

### The Real Bar

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>Your writing needs to be clear enough that a reader never has to re-read a sentence to understand what you meant.</span> Consistent, distracting errors — not just one typo — are what actually cost points, because they suggest the writer can't reliably control complex sentences, which undermines confidence in the *reasoning* too.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Key Takeaway</span>

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Analytical Writing rewards structure and reasoning speed under time pressure more than eloquence.</span> A clearly organized, moderately-written essay that addresses complexity will consistently outscore a beautifully written but one-sided or disorganized one.
