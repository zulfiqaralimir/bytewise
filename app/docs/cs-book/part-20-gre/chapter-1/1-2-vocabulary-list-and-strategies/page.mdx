# <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}><span style={{ color: "#059669" }}>The GregMat Vocabulary List</span>: 1,110 Words, and How to Actually Learn Them</span>

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>A vocabulary list by itself doesn't teach you anything — it's just a spreadsheet.</span> What follows is the full GregMat GRE word list (37 groups, roughly 1,110 words, plus a short bonus batch), organized the way it was originally grouped, followed by a concrete strategy for turning that spreadsheet into words you actually recognize under exam pressure.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Why a Word List Alone Won't Get You There</span>

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Reading a list top to bottom feels productive, but it mostly builds *recognition*, not *recall*.</span> Recognition means a word looks familiar when you see it. Recall means you can retrieve its meaning cold, under time pressure, buried inside an unfamiliar sentence — which is exactly what <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Text Completion and Sentence Equivalence</span> demand (see [Lesson 1.1](/docs/cs-book/part-20-gre/chapter-1/1-1-verbal-reasoning-decoded)).

The gap between recognition and recall is why so many test-takers <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>say "I've seen that word before" and still pick the wrong answer.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>The Learning Science: Three Principles That Actually Work</span>

- **Spaced repetition**: <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>review a word right after learning it, then a day later, then a week later, then a month later</span> — timed just before you'd naturally forget it. Cramming a group once and moving on guarantees you'll forget most of it within days.
- **Active recall**: <span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>test yourself on the word before checking the definition</span>, instead of just re-reading the word and its meaning. Forcing retrieval is what builds the memory; passive re-reading barely does.
- **Interleaving**: <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>mix old groups back into new study sessions</span> rather than fully mastering one group before touching the next. It feels harder in the moment, but it <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>produces retention that plain, one-group-at-a-time review never does.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Strategy 1: Learn Roots, Not Just Words</span>

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Many of these 1,110 words share Greek or Latin roots</span> — <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>learn the root once, and you get several words almost for free.</span> A few clusters that show up directly in this list:

- **pre- (before):** *prescient, presage, precipitate, precipitous, premeditate, precocious, prefigure, prescribe* — all carry a sense of "coming before" something.
- **mal- (bad):** *malign, malevolent, malfeasance* — all built on "bad/evil."
- **bene- (good):** *benign, beneficent, benevolent* — the direct opposite cluster.
- **a-/an- (without, Greek):** *amorphous* (without form), *apathy* (without feeling), *anomalous* (without a norm).
- **anti- (against/opposite):** *antithesis, antipathy, antedate, antagonize*.

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>When you hit an unfamiliar word on test day, scanning it for a root you recognize is often enough to narrow the meaning</span> — even if you never studied that exact word.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Strategy 2: Sort by Connotation, Not Just Definition</span>

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>A large share of GRE Verbal questions hinge on **tone** — does the blank need something positive, negative, or neutral?</span> — before they hinge on the precise dictionary definition (see the "Although" contrast pattern in [Lesson 1.1, Example 2](/docs/cs-book/part-20-gre/chapter-1/1-1-verbal-reasoning-decoded)). <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>You can often eliminate half the answer choices just by knowing whether a word is praise or criticism</span>, even before you recall its exact shade of meaning.

- **Positive-leaning:** *laudable, meritorious, sagacious, munificent, auspicious*
- **Negative-leaning:** *opprobrium, odious, egregious, perfidy, turpitude*

<span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>As you work through each group below, tag every word **+, –, or 0** (positive, negative, neutral) in your notes</span> before you even worry about the precise definition. <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>That single pass builds fast, exam-relevant intuition.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>Strategy 3: Write Your Own Text Completion Sentences</span>

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>The single best active-recall exercise for this list is writing your own Text Completion sentence for a new word</span>, using a <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>logic-signal word (*although, despite, because, consequently*)</span> to set up the blank — the exact pattern from Lesson 1.1.

> "The critic's review was uncharacteristically ______, given her usual reputation for scathing, unsparing commentary." *(answer: complimentary, generous, or similar — <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>the word "uncharacteristically" is the logic signal.</span>)*

<span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Do this for 3–5 words a day.</span> It forces you to actually understand the <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>word's connotation and usage, not just its dictionary definition.</span>

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>A 6-Week Study Schedule</span>

| Week | New Groups | Daily Load | Review Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Groups 1–7 | ~1 group/day (~30 words) | <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Same-day self-test + next-day recall check</span> |
| 2 | Groups 8–14 | ~1 group/day | <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Interleave 2–3 Week 1 groups every session</span> |
| 3 | Groups 15–21 | ~1 group/day | <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>Cumulative review of Weeks 1–2</span> |
| 4 | Groups 22–28 | ~1 group/day | Cumulative review of Weeks 1–3 |
| 5 | Groups 29–35 | ~1 group/day | Cumulative review of Weeks 1–4 |
| 6 | Groups 36–37 + bonus | Remaining words | <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Full cumulative review + mixed practice sets</span> |

<span style={{ color: "#059669", fontWeight: 700 }}>This pace covers all 37 groups in six weeks</span> while leaving room for the <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>spaced, interleaved review that actually locks the words in</span> — rather than a single fast pass that fades within days.

## <span style={{ fontFamily: '"Times New Roman", Times, serif' }}>How to Use This List Day-to-Day</span>

1. **Load each group into a spaced-repetition tool** — <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>Anki or Quizlet both work</span> — instead of just re-reading the spreadsheet. The software schedules your reviews automatically using the <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>spaced-repetition principle</span> above.
2. **Tag connotation (+/–/0) on first pass**, before memorizing exact definitions.
3. **Self-test before checking the answer** — cover the definition, try to recall it, then check.
4. **Write one original sentence** <span style={{ color: "#b45309", fontWeight: 700 }}>for any word that doesn't stick after two reviews.</span>
5. **Revisit every group at least three times** over the six weeks — <span style={{ color: "#7c3aed", fontWeight: 700 }}>first exposure, one-week-later review, and a final cumulative pass</span> before your test date.
