# 1.4: Frequency Count Method

---

## What Is the Frequency Count Method?

> The time taken by an algorithm can be found by assigning **one unit of time** to each simple statement, and finding the **frequency** — the number of times each statement actually executes. Summing "cost × frequency" across every statement gives the algorithm's **time function**, `f(n)`.

We'll apply this method to three algorithms of increasing complexity: summing the elements of an array, adding two matrices, and multiplying two matrices — and see the pattern of **O(n)**, **O(n²)**, and **O(n³)** emerge directly from the loop structure.

---

## Algorithm 1: Sum of Array Elements

Given an array `A` of size `n`, this algorithm adds up all its elements:

### Counting Frequencies

- `S = 0;` — a simple statement, executes **1** time.
- The `for` loop header has **three** parts, each with its own frequency:
  - `i = 0` — runs once: **1** time.
  - `i < n` — this condition is *checked* one more time than the body runs, because it's also checked the one final time it evaluates to false: **n + 1** times.
  - `i++` — runs once for every completed iteration: **n** times.
- `S = S + A[i];` — inside the loop body, executes **n** times.
- `return S;` — a simple statement, executes **1** time.

<Figure src="/images/freqcount-sum-array.svg" alt="Sum(A,n) algorithm annotated with frequency counts: S=0 runs 1 time, for-loop parts run 1, n+1, and n times, S=S+A[i] runs n times, return S runs 1 time, giving f(n)=2n+3, O(n)" width={760} height={340} caption="Every statement annotated with how many times it actually runs." />

### Time Function

Adding up every count: `1 + (1 + (n+1) + n) + n + 1`. The exact sum is `2n + 4`, but by convention the very small, constant loop-initialization pieces (`i=0`'s contribution) are folded together, and the lecture states it simply as:

$$f(n) = 2n + 3$$

The **degree** of this polynomial is **1** — so the time complexity is **O(n)**.

### Space Function

| Variable | A | n | S | i |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Space | n | 1 | 1 | 1 |

$$S(n) = n + 3$$

Also degree 1, so the space complexity is also **O(n)**.

## Algorithm 2: Sum of Two Matrices

`A` and `B` are square matrices of dimension `n × n` (e.g., 3×3). This algorithm computes their element-wise sum, `C`:

<Figure src="/images/freqcount-matrix-add.svg" alt="Add(A,B,n) algorithm annotated with frequency counts: outer for-loop runs n+1 times, inner for-loop runs n times (n+1) times, the assignment runs n times n times, giving f(n)=2n^2+2n+1, O(n^2), and space S(n)=3n^2+3, O(n^2)" width={780} height={320} caption="Nested loops multiply their frequencies together." />

### Counting Frequencies

- The **outer** `for (i...)` loop runs its header check **n + 1** times (same reasoning as before).
- The **inner** `for (j...)` loop runs its header check **n** times for *each* pass of the outer loop — giving **n × (n + 1)** total.
- The assignment `C[i,j] = A[i,j] + B[i,j];` sits inside *both* loops, so it runs **n × n** times.

### Time Function

$$f(n) = 2n^2 + 2n + 1$$

The highest degree term is **n²** — we don't write the exact coefficient, just note the **degree** of the polynomial. So the time complexity is **O(n²)**.

### Space Function

| Variable | A | B | C | n | i | j |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Space | n² | n² | n² | 1 | 1 | 1 |

$$S(n) = 3n^2 + 3$$

Both time and space complexity for this algorithm are **O(n²)**.

---

## Algorithm 3: Multiplication of Two Matrices

Matrix multiplication uses **three nested loops** instead of two — one extra level of nesting compared to matrix addition:

<Figure src="/images/freqcount-matrix-multiply.svg" alt="Multiply(A,B,n) algorithm annotated with frequency counts across three nested loops, giving f(n)=2n^3+3n^2+2n+1, O(n^3), and space S(n)=3n^2+4, O(n^2)" width={860} height={400} caption="A third nested loop multiplies the frequency by another factor of n, pushing the time complexity up to O(n³)." />

### Counting Frequencies

- **Outer** `for (i...)` loop: **n + 1** times.
- **Middle** `for (j...)` loop: **(n + 1) × n** times.
- `C[i,j] = 0;` (inside both `i` and `j` loops, but before the innermost loop): **n × n** times.
- **Innermost** `for (k...)` loop: **(n + 1) × n × n** times.
- The core statement `C[i,j] = C[i,j] + A[i,k]*B[k,j];`, nested inside *all three* loops: **n × n × n** times.

### Time Function

$$f(n) = 2n^3 + 3n^2 + 2n + 1$$

The degree of this polynomial is **3**, so the time complexity is **O(n³)**.

### Space Function

| Variable | A | B | C | n | i | j | k |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Space | n² | n² | n² | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

$$S(n) = 3n^2 + 4$$

> Notice that the **space** complexity stays at **O(n²)** even though the **time** complexity has grown to O(n³) — only the three n×n matrices dominate the space used; the extra loop variable `k` costs just one more constant unit of space, not an extra dimension.

---

## Summary: Three Algorithms, Three Complexity Classes

| Algorithm | Loop Structure | Time Complexity | Space Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sum of array elements** | 1 loop | O(n) | O(n) |
| **Sum of two matrices** | 2 nested loops | O(n²) | O(n²) |
| **Multiplication of two matrices** | 3 nested loops | O(n³) | O(n²) |

---

## The Big Picture

- The **Frequency Count Method** finds an algorithm's time function by assigning **1 unit of time** per simple statement and multiplying by how many times it actually **executes**.
- A single `for` loop that runs `n` times contributes an **n + 1** frequency for its condition check, and **n** for its body and increment — giving overall **O(n)** when the body does constant work.
- **Nesting loops multiplies their frequencies**: two nested loops over `n` give O(n²); three nested loops give O(n³).
- Once the time function `f(n)` is found, its time **complexity** is simply the **degree of the highest-order term** — exact coefficients and lower-order terms are dropped.
- **Space complexity** is found the same way, by adding up the space used by every variable (arrays cost their full size; scalars cost a constant 1) and again taking the degree of the resulting polynomial.
- Time and space complexity don't always match: matrix multiplication is O(n³) in time but only O(n²) in space, because the extra loop only adds a constant-size variable, not another array.

> **Simple takeaway:** count each statement's frequency, sum cost × frequency into f(n), and the degree of the highest-order term is the Big-O — nested loops are exactly what push that degree up.
