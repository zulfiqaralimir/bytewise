# Algorithms by Abdul Bari

## 1: Introduction to Algorithms

*Based on [Abdul Bari's Algorithms playlist](https://youtu.be/0IAPZzGSbME?si=yLoAVtKfYvm_5b8K).*

## Why This Subject Matters

**Algorithms** is a core subject in every computer science curriculum — and, honestly, one of the toughest for most students. That difficulty is exactly why it's worth taking seriously:

- **Competitive programming** contests draw the overwhelming majority of their questions from this subject.
- **Job interviews** — at nearly every software company — test algorithmic problem-solving directly.
- **Entrance and competitive exams** (GATE and similar) lean heavily on algorithm-based questions.

But the real payoff isn't any of those. The actual skill this subject builds is **logic development** — the ability to devise a strategy for solving a problem you've never seen before, rather than memorizing a fixed set of solutions. That skill transfers to every corner of engineering, not just the algorithms you'll study in this chapter.

> **A note on structure:** this chapter's lectures are numbered by *topic*, not strictly by upload order — large topics get broken into smaller sections. Follow the section numbers, not the sequence you happened to watch them in.

## What Is an Algorithm?

> **Definition:** An algorithm is a **step-by-step procedure for solving a computational problem.**

At first glance, a **program** looks like the same thing — also "a procedure for solving a problem." So what actually separates the two? Answering that precisely is the fastest way to understand what an algorithm really is.

---

## Algorithm vs. Program

### Two Phases of Software Development

Every engineered product — software included — goes through two major phases:

1. **Design phase** — plan and think through the solution thoroughly before building anything.
2. **Implementation phase** — actually build/construct it.

Unlike physical construction, where a mistake means demolishing and rebuilding, software is forgiving: a programmer can edit or delete code and start over almost for free. But that doesn't mean design should be skipped. Professional development still **designs first, then implements** — skipping straight to code is how you end up trial-and-error coding your way to a solution that's slow, wrong on edge cases, or both.

The algorithm is the artifact of the design phase. The program is the artifact of the implementation phase.

### The Core Distinction

| Aspect | Algorithm | Program |
|---|---|---|
| **Written at** | Design time | Implementation time |
| **Written in** | Plain English-like statements or math notation — no strict syntax | A specific programming language (C, C++, Java, Python, ...) with strict syntax |
| **Hardware/OS dependency** | **Independent** — no concern for machine or operating system | **Dependent** — must target specific hardware and OS (e.g. Linux vs. Windows) |
| **Written by** | Someone with strong **domain knowledge** of the problem — may or may not be a programmer | A **programmer** |
| **Verified by** | **Analysis** — studying it for correctness and efficiency (time and space) | **Testing** — running it and checking outputs |

**Think of it like** an architect's blueprint versus the finished building. The blueprint (algorithm) is language-agnostic — it doesn't care whether the contractor uses metric or imperial tools. The building (program) is very much tied to the specific materials and methods used to construct it. You verify a blueprint by *analyzing* it — does the load-bearing math work out? You verify a building by *testing* it — does it actually stand?

### Who Writes an Algorithm?

Ideally, the person with the **domain knowledge** of the problem area — not necessarily the person who will eventually write the code.

- An **accountant** understands accounting workflows better than most programmers ever will.
- **Hospital staff and doctors** understand clinical workflows better than the engineer building the hospital's scheduling system.

A programmer *can* also be the algorithm designer, if they have sufficient domain knowledge of the problem — the same person can act as both **designer** and **programmer**. But the two roles are conceptually distinct, and on large systems they're often literally different people: a domain expert designs the logic, and an engineer implements it.

---

## What Language Is Used to Write an Algorithm?

Any language works — plain **English**, or **mathematical notation**. The only hard requirement: it must be **understandable to everyone who needs to use it**. If a domain expert writes the algorithm, the programmer who eventually implements it has to be able to read it.

**In practice**, since most computer science students learn C early in their education, algorithms are commonly written using **C-style pseudocode**. This sidesteps the confusion of introducing an unfamiliar notation, and it's understandable across the widest possible audience — C-style syntax (`if`, `for`, `while`, `=`, `==`) is close enough to universal that most programmers can read it regardless of which language they actually work in.

> This chapter will express algorithms using **C-style pseudocode**, for exactly that reason: it's the convention most widely understood across students and working engineers alike.

---

## The Big Picture

- An **algorithm** is a step-by-step solution written at **design time** — language- and platform-independent.
- A **program** is the implementation of that algorithm in a specific programming language, tied to specific hardware and OS.
- Algorithms are **analyzed** for correctness and efficiency (time and space complexity); programs are **tested** by running them.
- Algorithms should ideally be written by whoever best understands the **problem domain** — that person may or may not be the programmer.
- This chapter expresses algorithms in **C-style pseudocode**, since it's the notation most widely understood across audiences.

> **Simple takeaway:** an algorithm is the *thinking*, written down and made language-independent; a program is that thinking, translated into a specific language and *tested* rather than merely analyzed.
