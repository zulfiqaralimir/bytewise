## What Is a Strategy?

> **Definition:** A strategy is an **approach or design** for solving a computational problem.

- There is **no fixed formula** that tells you exactly which strategy applies to which problem.
- Recognizing when a strategy fits a problem comes largely from **practice**, guided by a few general guidelines.

---

## The Core Idea of Divide and Conquer

Suppose a problem **P** has an input of size **n**, and this problem is considered **too large** to solve directly. The strategy proceeds as follows:

1. **Divide:** Break the large problem `P` into smaller subproblems `P1, P2, P3, ... Pk`.
2. **Conquer (Solve):** Solve each subproblem individually to get solutions `Solution1, Solution2, ... SolutionK`.
3. **Combine:** Merge these individual solutions together to form the solution for the original main problem `P`.

If any subproblem is *itself* still too large, the same three-step process is applied to it again — this is what makes divide and conquer algorithms fundamentally **recursive** in nature.

---

## The Critical Rule: Subproblems Must Match the Original Problem

> **Rule:** Every subproblem created by dividing must be the **same type of problem** as the original — it cannot turn into a different kind of task.

### Valid Example

- If the original problem is **"sort this data,"** then each subproblem must also be **"sort a (smaller) piece of data."**
- This qualifies as divide and conquer.

### Invalid Example (Not Divide and Conquer)

- Consider organizing a **college workshop**. This large task might be broken into: preparing invitations, preparing posters, inviting guests, and arranging resource persons.
- Each of these is a **different kind of task** — none of them is "organize a smaller workshop."
- **This is not divide and conquer** — it's simply task decomposition, not the recursive same-problem-on-smaller-input pattern.

---

## You Also Need a Way to Combine Solutions

Beyond just splitting the problem, you must also have a **method to combine** the subproblem solutions back into a solution for the main problem. If no such combining method exists, the divide and conquer strategy cannot be applied, no matter how naturally the problem seems to split.

> **Two-part checklist** for deciding whether divide and conquer applies to a problem:
> 1. When divided, do the subproblems remain the **same type** of problem as the original?
> 2. Do you have a **method to combine** the subproblem solutions into the main solution?
>
> If both are true, divide and conquer is applicable.

---

## The General Method

```
DivideAndConquer(P):
    if P is small:
        solve P directly
    else:
        divide P into subproblems P1, P2, ..., Pk
        for each Pi:
            apply DivideAndConquer(Pi)      // recursive call
        combine the solutions of P1, P2, ..., Pk
        to form the solution of P
```

- **Base case:** if the problem is small enough, solve it **directly** — a solution must always exist for the smallest case.
- **Recursive case:** if the problem is large, divide → recursively conquer each part → combine the results.
- This structure is why divide-and-conquer **algorithms and procedures are inherently recursive.**

## The Big Picture

- A **strategy** is a general approach for solving computational problems — divide and conquer is the first of several strategies in this course.
- Divide and conquer works in **three steps: Divide → Conquer (solve) → Combine.**
- Subproblems must be the **same type** of problem as the original (e.g., sorting → sorting), not just any decomposition of work.
- A valid **combining method** must exist, or the strategy cannot be applied.
- Divide-and-conquer algorithms are **recursive by nature.**
- Classic problems in this topic: **Binary Search, Max/Min, Merge Sort, Quick Sort, Strassen's Matrix Multiplication.**

> **Simple takeaway:** divide and conquer isn't just "break the problem into pieces" — it only counts if the pieces are the same kind of problem, smaller, and you have a way to stitch their answers back together.
