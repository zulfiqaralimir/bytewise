# Lesson 12 — Distributed Transactions

We now move from **conflict resolution** to one of the most important problems in distributed systems:

> **What happens when one business operation needs to update multiple independent databases or services?**

## 1. First: What Is a Transaction?

A **transaction** is a group of operations that should behave as one logical unit. Transferring Rs. 10,000 means `Account A: -10,000` and `Account B: +10,000` — we don't want A debited while B never receives it. We want **all-or-nothing** behavior. A traditional database gives us the **ACID** properties: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability.

## 2. Local Transaction vs Distributed Transaction

A **local transaction** happens inside one database, which can control the whole thing itself.

This is why distributed systems heavily use **idempotency** mechanisms such as idempotency keys for operations that may be retried.

## 9. Approach 2 — Saga

A **Saga** takes a different approach: instead of one giant open transaction, break the work into local transactions that each commit independently, and if something later fails, run a **compensating transaction**.

> **Rollback = undo the transaction. Compensation = perform an action that undoes the business effect.**

## 11. Distributed Transactions and Isolation

Suppose stock is `1` and two customers both try to buy it. If both services independently see `Stock = 1` and both reserve successfully, we now have one item and two successful reservations. So distributed transactions aren't just about "did everything commit?" — they also involve concurrency, isolation, ordering, locking, retries, duplicate requests, and failure recovery.

## 12–14. Distributed Transactions vs Locks, Consensus, and Replication

A **distributed lock** answers *"who is allowed to access this resource right now?"* A **distributed transaction** answers *"how do multiple independent operations succeed or recover as one business operation?"* A transaction may use locks, but a lock by itself doesn't create a transaction.

**Consensus** asks *"what value/decision should the distributed nodes agree on?"* A distributed transaction asks *"how do multiple resource changes achieve the required business consistency?"* Consensus can be used inside infrastructure supporting transactions, but consensus ≠ distributed transaction — and 2PC specifically is an atomic-commit protocol, not simply general consensus.

**Replication** answers *"how do I maintain multiple copies?"* A distributed transaction answers *"how do I coordinate a multi-resource business operation?"*

## 15. The E-Commerce Example

A `BUY` click needs: create order, reserve inventory, charge payment, create shipment — spanning an Order DB, Inventory DB, and Payment DB behind a payment gateway. If `Order ✓, Inventory ✓, Payment ✓, Shipment ✗`, the system needs a recovery strategy: **2PC** (atomic commit) or **Saga** (compensation).

## 16. Why Sagas Become Important

Imagine a transaction lasting 30 minutes — flight, hotel, car rental, insurance. Keeping distributed locks/resources held for the entire process is undesirable. A Saga instead lets each booking commit independently: if the car booking fails after flight and hotel succeeded, compensate with `Cancel hotel → Release flight`. This allows each local transaction to complete independently while the overall process recovers through compensating actions — but remember, **Saga does NOT give the same atomicity as one ACID transaction**; there can be a temporary inconsistent state while compensation is happening.

## 18. The Three Most Important Ideas

**Partial failure** — some operations can succeed while others fail (`✓ ✓ ✗ ✓`). **Unknown outcome** — a timeout doesn't tell you whether the operation actually happened. **Compensation** — sometimes you cannot literally roll back, so instead you issue a new action (like a refund) that reverses the business effect.

## 19. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1 — "Distributed transaction means calling several APIs."** Not necessarily — the important part is the coordination and consistency requirements across independent resources.

**Mistake 2 — "A Saga gives atomicity."** No — Saga manages multi-step workflows using local transactions and compensation, generally accepting eventual consistency.

**Mistake 3 — "Timeout means the operation failed."** No — it may have succeeded but the response was lost.

**Mistake 4 — "Compensation is rollback."** Not exactly — rollback undoes uncommitted state; compensation is a new transaction that reverses a committed business effect.

**Mistake 5 — "Retry everything."** Dangerous — a retry can duplicate an operation (`Charge $100 → timeout → retry → another charge`). Use idempotency where appropriate.

## 21. Memory Trick

> **Distributed Transaction = One business operation across multiple independent systems.**

`2PC: Prepare → Commit/Abort`, while `Saga: Local Transactions → Compensation`.

> **Rollback undoes uncommitted work. Compensation undoes the business effect of already-committed work.**

## Interview Questions

**Q1. What is a distributed transaction?**
> A transaction whose operations span multiple independent services, databases, or resource managers and therefore require coordination.

**Q2. Why are distributed transactions difficult?**
> Because components can fail independently, messages can be delayed/lost, and there is no single local database transaction controlling all resources.

**Q3. What is partial failure?**
> A situation where some components succeed while others fail during the same logical operation.

**Q4. Is timeout equal to failure?**
> No — a timeout can mean the operation's outcome is unknown.

**Q5. What is the difference between rollback and compensation?**
> Rollback reverses uncommitted transactional work; compensation is a new operation designed to reverse a previously committed business effect.

**Q6. What are the two major approaches we'll study?**
> Two-Phase Commit (2PC) and the Saga Pattern.

**Q7. Does Saga provide true atomicity?**
> No — Saga uses local transactions and compensating actions, generally providing eventual rather than global ACID atomicity.

**One-line exam answer:**

> **A distributed transaction is a logical business transaction whose operations span multiple independent services or databases and therefore require coordination to maintain the required consistency and failure-handling guarantees.**
