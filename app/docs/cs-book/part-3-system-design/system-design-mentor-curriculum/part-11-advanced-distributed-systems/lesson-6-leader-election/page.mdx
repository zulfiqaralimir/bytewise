## 1. What Is Leader Election?

**Leader election** is the process by which nodes in a distributed system **agree on which node should become the leader**.

## 2. Why Do We Need a Leader?

A leader can simplify coordination. Imagine five servers all independently trying to coordinate writes:

```text
A → write X
B → write Y
C → write Z
```

Now everyone has to determine which operation comes first. Instead, we can choose one coordinator:

## 3. Real-Life Analogy

Imagine a classroom with 30 students. If every student tries to coordinate the class, it's chaos — *"Do this!" "No, do this!" "Wait!" "I decide!"* Instead, the class selects one representative who coordinates communication. But what happens if the leader leaves? The class needs to select another leader. That's leader election.

## 4. Leader Election Is Different From Consensus

These concepts are closely related but not identical.

**Consensus** asks: *how do distributed nodes agree on a decision?*

**Leader Election** asks: *how do distributed nodes agree on who the leader should be?*

## 6. Why Can't a Follower Just Become Leader?

Suppose A is leader and disappears. B might say *"I'm the leader now,"* while C simultaneously says *"No, I'm the leader."* Now we have **two leaders**. This is called **split brain**, and it can be extremely dangerous. Therefore, leader election needs rules that prevent conflicting leaders from being accepted.

## 7. Split Brain

Imagine A is the leader of B and C, and the network partitions so B and C can't reach each other. If B thinks A has failed, and C also thinks A has failed, and both independently become leaders:

## 8. How Does Raft Prevent Split Brain?

Raft uses **terms and majority voting**. Suppose we have five nodes, `A B C D E`. A leader fails and B becomes candidate. B needs a majority — `3 / 5`. If B gets `B ✓ C ✓ D ✓`, that's `3/5`, so B can become leader. Now imagine E independently tries to become leader but only has itself — `1/5`. Not enough. Therefore:

> **A candidate needs the required quorum/majority to establish leadership.**

## 9. Why Majority Helps

Suppose there are five nodes and majority is 3. One candidate might get `A B C`. Another candidate cannot simultaneously obtain a separate majority without overlapping with that first group. This overlap is important — it makes it much harder for two conflicting candidates to both legitimately obtain majority support in the same election term.

## 11. Why Use Randomized Election Timeouts?

Imagine B, C, and D all detect the leader failure at exactly the same moment and all become candidates. Votes may then be divided — `B → 2 votes`, `C → 2 votes`, `D → 1 vote` — and no candidate has a majority. This is called a **split vote**.

Randomized election timeouts reduce the probability that many nodes start elections simultaneously:

```text
B → timeout = 150 ms
C → timeout = 230 ms
D → timeout = 190 ms
```

B starts first. It may obtain votes before the others start elections.

## 13. The Election Process

Let's put everything together. Suppose A is leader and B, C, D, E are followers.

**Step 1 — Leader fails:** `A ❌`

**Step 2 — Followers wait:** each of B, C, D, E is running its own timer.

**Step 3 — One timeout occurs first:** `B → timeout`

**Step 4 — B becomes Candidate.**

**Step 5 — B requests votes:** `B → C: Vote for me?`, `B → D: Vote for me?`, `B → E: Vote for me?`

**Step 6 — Majority:** suppose `B ✓ C ✓ D ✓` — that's `3 / 5`.

**Step 7 — B becomes leader:**

## 14. What Happens to the Old Leader?

Suppose A wasn't actually dead — maybe there was only a temporary network problem. After recovering, A discovers the current term (say `8`) is newer than what it remembers (`7`). A learns that its information is outdated. It steps down and becomes a follower. This is one reason **terms** are so important.

## 15. Leader Election and Terms

Recall from Raft: each election is associated with a term.

| Term | Leader |
|---|---|
| 1 | Leader A |
| 2 | Leader B |
| 3 | Leader C |

A node can use the term number to recognize stale leadership information. If Node A knows `term = 5` and Node B says `term = 7`, A knows B's information is from a newer term.

## 16. Leader Election Is Not Just About Raft

Leader election is a general distributed-systems problem. It can be used whenever a system needs one node to coordinate an operation:

- **Distributed databases** — a leader coordinates writes.
- **Distributed coordination** — a leader coordinates cluster state.
- **Job scheduling** — a leader assigns jobs.
- **Distributed locks** — a leader/coordinator coordinates ownership.
- **Replicated state machines** — a leader orders commands.

## 17. Leader vs Primary

You may hear different terminology — Leader, Primary, Master, Coordinator. These words can have different meanings depending on the system. In many distributed databases, a **Primary** accepts writes and replicates to **Replicas**. In Raft, a **Leader** replicates its log to **Followers**. They can perform similar coordination roles, but **don't assume the words are universally interchangeable**.

## 18. Leader Election vs Load Balancing

**Load balancing** asks: *which server should handle this request?* — requests are distributed across multiple servers.

**Leader election** asks: *which node should be the coordinator/leader?*

So:

> **Load balancing distributes requests. Leader election chooses a coordinator.**

## 19. Leader Election vs DHT

Remember Lesson 2. **DHT** asks *where is the data?* — key → responsible node. **Leader election** asks *who should coordinate?* — nodes → election → leader. Very different problems.

## 20. Leader Election vs Consensus

This distinction is especially important. **Consensus** is the general agreement problem. **Leader election** is agreement about leadership specifically. Leader election can be **part of a consensus algorithm** — inside Raft, it sits alongside log replication and commitment.

## 21. Programming Connection

A simplified node might have:

```python
role = "follower"
term = 5
votes = 0
```

When timeout occurs:

```python
role = "candidate"
term += 1
votes = 1
```

Then the candidate requests votes with `requestVote()`. If majority is reached, `role = "leader"`, and the leader begins sending heartbeats with `heartbeat()`. This is only a conceptual model; a real implementation needs persistent state, network messaging, timers, concurrency, and careful protocol rules.

## 22. DSA Connection

Leader election uses several familiar concepts.

- **Counters** — `votes = 3`
- **Sets** — track who has voted: `{B, C, D}`
- **State machines** — `FOLLOWER → CANDIDATE → LEADER`
- **Timers** — `election_timeout`, `heartbeat_interval`
- **Networking** — `send_vote_request()`, `receive_vote()`, `send_heartbeat()`

This is another excellent example of:

> **DSA + networking + distributed algorithms = distributed systems.**

## 23. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1 — "Leader election means finding the fastest server."** No. It means selecting a node according to the distributed protocol's rules.

**Mistake 2 — "The leader is permanently fixed."** No. Leadership can change because of failures, elections, configuration changes, or other protocol events.

**Mistake 3 — "A node becomes leader as soon as the old leader disappears."** No. It normally must go through the election process and obtain the required support.

**Mistake 4 — "Two leaders can never temporarily exist."** In real distributed systems, temporary conflicting perceptions can occur during failures/partitions; protocols such as Raft use terms and quorum rules to prevent conflicting leaders from safely committing conflicting decisions.

**Mistake 5 — "Leader election and load balancing are the same."** No — load balancing distributes requests, leader election selects a coordinator.

## 24. Interview Questions

**Q1. What is leader election?**
> Leader election is the process by which distributed nodes select one node to act as the coordinator or leader.

**Q2. Why do distributed systems need leader election?**
> A leader can simplify coordination, ordering, replication, and decision-making among distributed nodes.

**Q3. What happens when a Raft leader fails?**
> Followers eventually time out, one becomes a candidate, requests votes, and can become leader after obtaining a majority.

**Q4. What is split brain?**
> A condition where different parts of a distributed system believe they have independent leadership or authority.

**Q5. Why does Raft use randomized election timeouts?**
> To reduce the probability that multiple followers become candidates simultaneously and split the vote.

**Q6. Why are terms important?**
> Terms allow nodes to distinguish newer leadership/election information from stale information.

**Q7. What happens if there is no quorum?**
> The system cannot safely establish/commit new consensus decisions, so it may sacrifice availability for safety.
